Title to be discussed collectively
ADAPT-Nan - Advancing Development of Agroecology in Nan Province
Publication Plan - 2026

Abstract
The book reports on the key lessons of ADAPT Nan, a multi-year initiative dedicated to advancing agroecology and sustainable land management in Thailand’s Nan Province. The book explores the region's transition from chemical-intensive monocultures toward integrated farming systems that address "wicked" problems like deforestation, indebtedness, and environmental degradation. By examining case studies ranging from organic vegetable networks and native cattle preservation to youth biodiversity programs, the sources highlight how scientific knowledge and local wisdom can foster socio-ecological resilience. The chapters document a fifty-year history of civil society movements, emphasizing the importance of learning centres, intergenerational knowledge, and experiential education in building a sustainable future. Ultimately, the book serves as both a research synthesis and a strategic roadmap for scaling agroecological transitions across Southeast Asia.

2.1. Organic vegetable networks - Participatory Guarantee System in Nan
Who are the organic farmers in Nan Province? Can they live comfortably from their farming activities, or do they need additional income generating activities to sustain their organic farm? What is the future of organic farming in Nan Province and what would be the enabling conditions of such a future? How does organic farming link with ecotourism potential of the northern upland province. We will address these questions by investigating the past and the present of organic farming and then by mobilizing this knowledge to explore possible futures with key stakeholders. 
Going organic is not only a question of agronomic practices or economic development. In this study we investigated the multiple dimensions of organic agriculture, including the underlying values of engagement in sustainable agriculture and the challenges related to the marketing of clean products (certification, competition, etc.). Going organic is also a lifestyle beyond agricultural practices. We studied the diversity of pathways that brought individuals or collectives to different models of organic farming and explored the diversity of organic farms in Nan province.
Through this study we stimulated debates involving local communities, policy makers and civil society about the future of food systems in Nan Province. We also analyzed the role of scientific knowledge in supporting a desirable future and provided guidance to university engagement in community development.
Methods
Secondary data collection and analysis about organic farming and sustainable agriculture in Nan Province. List support projects to agroecology and organic farming over the years. Identify outstanding stories, actors, farms, collectives. Mapping of initiatives: timeframe, locations, crops involved (rice, vegetable, coffee, etc.). 
Documentation of two emblematic cases of vegetables organic networks: PGS Nan and SDG-PGS. Questionnaire surveys with resource persons, individual interviews, focus group discussions on different locations with prominent stories related to sustainable agriculture, including a diversity of intervention mechanisms and actor-networks. 
Data analysis, reporting and foresight. Organization of a one-day workshop to deliver the results of the study to key stakeholders. Exploration of organic futures in Nan Province.
Results
i) a typology of organic farms in Nan province illustrating their multiple development pathways, the role of PGS initiatives in organizing producers and production in relation to market demand,
ii) lessons learnt in terms of enabling conditions to organic farming and how scientific knowledge may support an organic movement, and 
iii) exploring the future of food systems and PGS mechanisms in Nan Province based on past experiences, based on the analysis of social networks of organic farmers.


2.2. Native Red Cows - Preserving Nan natural and cultural heritage
The purpose of the study on Native Nan Cow is to assess the impact pathway of Chulalongkorn University Center for Social Engagement (CU-CSE) in Nan. The red cow landrace is a domesticated, locally adapted, native species of cattle that has developed over time, through adaptation to its natural and cultural environment, and due to isolation from other populations of the species. A research program started 10 years ago to characterize the genetic strain of the Nan red cow and then to preserve this traditional landrace as an agricultural heritage of Nan Province. A genetic management program was launched at the Lai Nan campus of Chulalongkorn University in Wiang Sa District, Nan Province. Then a development program involved ex-situ management of this traditional landrace by local communities. Besides training sessions, some pure breed cows were given to members of a farmers’ network who shared the calves born to other members of the network to gradually improve the genetics of the native cow population.
The “outcome evidencing” study investigated the transformative role of the scientific knowledge mobilized in this intervention. It also explored the benefits and constraints related to the different intervention mechanisms and to see to what extent they can support the long-term community engagement. The study feeds contemporary scientific debates about transformative research and the role of scientific knowledge in supporting local development.
Methods: Secondary data collection and analysis about the preservation of Nan native red cows. Questionnaire surveys and focus groups discussions. Data analysis and reporting.
Results: Preserving Nan natural and cultural heritage through its emblematic cow breed.

2.3. La Oon Nan – Sensitizing the youth to the role and importance of biodiversity
Scientific research conducted on community forests in Nan Province have revealed the value of the ecosystem services they provide. However, this rich body of knowledge still needed to be passed on to the younger generation – to drive their engagement in forest conservation. 
The research project entitled “Community Forestry Knowledge Management Using a Companion Modeling Approach in Nan Province” was implemented by the Faculty of Science of Chulalongkorn University and hosted by CU-CSE. It created shared learning among youths about community forest management. On the longer term, the research aimed to enhance community-driven natural resource conservation as community forests were becoming increasingly under threat from encroachment and deforestation.
The participatory action-research comprised three main stages: (i) sensitization of school children and local youths on forest multiple values through gaming simulation approaches, (ii) involved community members in monitoring forest resources through direct measurements of carbon and biodiversity, and (iii) shared the results with community members and local authorities through creative communication approaches.
The study of “La Oon Nan” assessed the impact pathway of the transformative pedagogy that was applied by the action-research described here above over a ten-year period in Nan Province. Besides the investigation of the benefits for local communities and forest resources, we document the diversity of impact pathways through behavioral changes of the youth involved in the project.
Methods
Synthesis of action-research results and secondary data analysis from the multiple games-simulations implemented over the years. Mapping of simulation results: identification of the study sites in Phratha, Chiang Klang, Thawang Pha, and/or Sa Districts and resource persons from different stakeholder groups involved
Questionnaire surveys of resource persons through individual interviews (community members and local authorities), one year after and 5 years after participation of school students and teachers, organization of focus group discussions in different locations where the projects have been implemented over the years. Data analysis and reporting.

3.1. Learning centers as islands of agroecological coherence
Sustainable agriculture has long been supported by multiple actor networks that operate over a large range of intervention mechanisms. In the context of Nan Province, different sectors, such as private enterprises, government agencies, royal projects, scientific researchers, Buddhist temples, individual farmers or local communities are involved in agricultural extension projects that support alternative agroecological practices. Some initiatives are time bound; others are deeply rooted in specific locations and durably engaged with local communities. 
We consider these learning centers, or living labs, as seeds for change as they offer concrete support to experiential learning beyond the traditional activities of agricultural extension services. These latter have largely contributed to the Green Revolution and the spread of intensive agriculture models. They are based on innovation by imitation while the former are linked to an emergence process resulting from the meeting of specific people and places. Indeed, agroecology emerges with different agricultural practices and crop-livestock-forest combinations that meet local people’s desires and capacities with the particular socio-ecological context in the location where they have settled. 
Methods: 
Survey of a sample of “learning centers” distributed across the Nan Province. Description of its historical pathway, social network, practices, business model. The grid for comparative analysis of the learning center emerges from the documentation of each story and the personality of the people behind (charismatic leaders – hero journeys) and characteristics of the local environments.
After studying the binding dimensions of each learning center (what keeps the cohesion of each center through the binding relations of its members) we study the bridging elements that link the centers to the outside world and possibly to other centers. We study social networks and higher-level coalitions that may synergize the action of several centers toward an agroecology movement.
Results: 
A mapping and description of learning centers across the Nan province with key data and infographics
A comparative analysis of the different models of emergence and conditions for sustainability
A network analysis of interactions between learning centers and intergenerational transmission

3.2. From community engagement to civil society movement - a 50-year story
Agroecology definitions traditionally distinguish three interrelated dimensions: science, practice and movement. This chapter addresses the third dimension of social engagement toward an agroecological transition. That means going beyond demonstrations and prototyping of alternative agricultural practices to broad-based transformations engaging the whole society.
Nam province has a long history of civil society movement from 1975 to 1993, through three emblematic figures: Por Pin Inthali — the forest conservation leader who in 1975 led 500 villagers to physically block trucks carrying 3,600 felled trees, triggering national media coverage ; Phra Khru Phithak Nantakhun — the Buddhist monk who opposed eucalyptus planting on public lands, organised the first aquatic animal spirit ceremonies (1990–1993), and became the spiritual and organisational anchor of the Raks Mueang Nan network. Dr. Bunyong Wongsrikamit — director of Nan Hospital for 30 years (1964–1993), who during the communist insurgency (1968–1983) built an extraordinary culture of service, sacrifice, and community trust under extreme resource scarcity. 
Several scholars have analysed the factors that supported the province-wide movement, including the roles played by model leaders, learning culture, opportunity creation, network expansion methods, and communication atmosphere. They emphasized cross-sector collaboration between community, government, business, NGOs, and academics for sustainable development. They highlighted five analytical factors: diversity that supports cross-sector collaborations; firsthand observation and storytelling from experience; rituals and ceremonies that foster a spiritual cement; the chaordic organisational culture of the Raks Mueang Nan group (volunteering, equality, transparency, flexibility); and Wethee adaptive spaces enabling knowledge co-creation.
Many lessons learnt may support the emergence of an agroecology movement. They deserve full recognition. For example, the Research Companion (RC) model introduced by TRF involved communities into research program in transdisciplinary processes in Nan Province as soon as 2000–2003. The Chavana Farmers' School at Ban Thung Mong with a 18-week curriculum promoting knowledge return to community and peer learning without formal teachers. Water and natural resource management conflicts were also addressed through the neutral facilitating role of the coordination centre, by engaging all parties in a fruitful dialogue. Creating space for system thinking and exchanges, the Wethee (เวที — community forum) methodology combined with mind mapping as a recording tool contributed to the success and sustainability of the community movement, contributing to the "Nan-Style Knowledge Management". 
The Nan movement was not planned. It is made up of islands (learning centres) that emerged independently, each driven by distinct leaders and distinct problems, before discovering their solidarity with one another. They were pockets of coherence within a landscape dominated by other logics. The Coordination Centre has operated without a membership list, without a hierarchy, without its own budget. This organisational “fragility” was also a resource for resilience and transmissions of key values to the next generation of leaders.
Based on literature review and interviews of key resource persons, this paper points to the legacy of former civil society movements, how they shape current policy dialogues and community engagements with new actors such as Raks Pa Nan foundation, and what perspective they draw for the future of Nan civil society and the emergence of an agroecology movement. 
3.3. Experiential learning for advancing the development of agroecology
In the context of Nan Province, as described in the previous chapters, we have shown the key roles of: (i) strong leadership driven by higher values and principles, (ii) adaptive spaces favoring synergies across multiple sectors, and (iii) governance models supportive to knowledge co-creation. They are crucial elements of an agroecological transition capable of dealing with the long list of wicked entangled problems that have persisted in time. Some decades ago, Nan Province could benefit from all these resources, but since then the context has changed, individualistic behaviors have increased and the next generation has massively left the province in search of a better life. The sense of “Being Nan” that was so strong in the early 2000s may have been lost in the meantime. 
Today, a large part of the farmers is still dependent on intensive monocropping for their survival and many of them are still trapped in the vicious circle of indebtedness. While this agricultural population is ageing and intensive monocropping is still covering large areas of the province, new hopes come in the form of “newcomers” who develop activities better aligned with the principles of agroecology. These people are coming back home to take care of their aging parents after experiencing corporate life in Bangkok or non-agricultural activities in other provinces of the country, others are people who came as tourists, where enchanted by the prospect of living in harmony with nature, and decide to settle there, then the third group is made of the youths who grew up in Nan and decided to stay there to take over their parent’s farm and improve its management. In all these cases, intergenerational solidarity, experiential education and transmission agroecological principles are key elements of agroecological transition.
We have shown the important role of universities and higher education institutions in community engagement outside of the university itself. In Nan, one of the first movements of civil society, back in the 1990s, was a call for a university to settle in the province. In this chapter, we present the process of co-designing two agroecological curriculum dedicated to three types of learners: high school students, community members and university students. We present the successive steps we went through in sensing the socio-ecological system, co-creating relevant knowledge and engaging in experiential pedagogy. The resulting curricula have been tested in 2026, and we report here about preliminary assessments by participants and organizers. The impact pathway of the ADAPT project is finally assessed using an evaluation framework based on individual surveys and focus group discussions.
