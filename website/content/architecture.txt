Advancing Development of Agroecology in Nan Province in Thailand

Project ADAPT-Nan in a Glance
Our Intentions (บริบทของการเกษตร) 
Transitioning from monocropping and high chemical use (maize, rubber) to sustainable, integrated systems (rice, vegetables, tea, cocoa, coffee, and herbs) to ensure food sovereignty and security.
Agroecology is seen as a solution to addressing key environmental issues (ความท้าทายด้านสิ่งแวดล้อม):
Climate change mitigation and adaptation
Water management: drought and flood (การจัดการน้ำ).
Haze and air quality: biomass burning and PM 2.5 (การลดฝุ่นควัน).
Biodiversity: forest management and ecosystem health (การอนุรักษ์ความหลากหลายทางชีวภาพ).
Chemicals use in agriculture and safe-food systems.
Our Goals (วัตถุประสงค์ของโครงการ) 
Promoting participatory research and transformative teaching that supports agroecology practitioners and stakeholders through co-designed experiential learning. ส่งเสริมการวิจัยแบบมีส่วนร่วมและการสอนแบบเปลี่ยนแปลงรูปแบบ เพื่อสนับสนุนผู้ปฏิบัติงานและผู้มีส่วนได้ส่วนเสียในด้านระบบนิเวศเกษตร
Educational Pillars
1. Higher Education (ระดับอุดมศึกษา)
Agroecology BSc Curriculum: An international program featuring immersive field blocks in Nan.
Experiential Learning: Integrating scientific theory with community-based applications to develop environmental stewards.
2. Secondary Schools (โรงเรียนมัธยมปลาย)
Hands-on Training: Practical workshops for high school students on socio-ecological issues and agroecological principles.
Engagement: Utilizing simulation games and farm visits to inspire the next generation of farmers.
3. Farmer & Community Support (เกษตรกรและชุมชน)
Transition Support: Providing counseling and training for organic farmers’ groups and local agricultural networks.
Knowledge Sharing: Hosting Nan Forums and developing online learning media for diverse stakeholders.
Our Approach (แนวทางดำเนินงาน)
Participatory Tools: Direct observation, focus groups, and role-play games.
Systems Thinking: Encouraging students to navigate complex socio-ecological challenges.
Local Wisdom: Valuing farmer knowledge alongside scientific innovation.

Description
Background
In Thailand and more broadly in Southeast Asia, the agricultural population is aging, which creates major challenges. First, for many elderly farmers (the average age of farmers is nearly 60 years while it was 55 years a decade ago), agriculture has become a supplement to other sources of income. Second, many elderly farmers are accustomed to intensive agricultural practices that are not environmentally friendly, and children do not wish to take over the farm after their parents' retirement. To re-enchant agriculture for younger generations and guide them toward agroecological practices aligned with environmental respect values, it is now necessary to move away from traditional production and knowledge transfer methods and promote knowledge co-creation processes with tomorrow's agricultural actors.
Agroecology reduces climate risks and promotes sustainable food systems. Mixed agricultural systems that integrate trees with crops and livestock, for example, are present in about 70% of Southeast Asian farms and are essential to rural populations' livelihoods. The engagement of agroecology actors is based on deep values of farmers' and agricultural products' quality of life, human and ecosystem health, solidarity and knowledge sharing rather than purely economic logic. The goal is to develop alternative forms of knowledge co-production and sharing, which is directly useful for people who wish to implement agroecology principles. The development of experiential training modules and their availability to high school teachers is a high priority investment for Thai universities.
Objectives
The project objective is to promote participatory research and transformative pedagogy in support of agroecology actors in Nan province, Thailand. The project's direct beneficiaries are high schools’ teachers in Nan province who will be engaged in the program as well as their students who will participate in knowledge co-production activities on agroecological transition issues. Young farmers who participate in training workshops benefit from personalized support in implementing agroecological practices on their farms. University students who accompany these participatory systems also acquire knowledge in sustainability science and know-how in terms of facilitating participatory processes. 
Activities
Development of experiential training on agroecology issues, principles, and practices for high school teachers and students in Nan province, Thailand.
The module is co-developed by university professors and high school teachers through successive tests with high school students designed to raise awareness about agroecology and sustainable food issues.
The training module is video recorded for integration into an e-learning system available on a digital platform. It can be mobilized by other agroecology initiatives in the country and regionally.
Making pedagogical material available to teachers and trainers who wish to engage in alternative pedagogies supporting the agroecological transition.
An online database of serious games and facilitation tools is available to support participatory workshops.
An online manual reports on the project's main results. It can be used by trainers as a teaching base.

Research

1. Agrarian change
1.1. Agricultural transformations and environmental issues
Investigating drivers of change, dimensions of change and local adaptation mechanisms.
Methods: Literature review, interviews of key informants, statistical data.
Changing landscapes - modernization of agriculture but persistence of environmental issues: 
Water scarcity (droughts) and abundance (floods)
Forest and agricultural residues burning (haze and PM 2.5)
Adaptation to climate change and mitigation 
Changing livelihoods - aging farmers, decreasing population and newcomers in search of Nan specific identity (nature and culture)
Tensions about tenure rights and forest policies
Indebtedness and constraints to investments in sustainable agriculture
New technologies (drones, mechanization, etc.)
1.2. Toxic landscapes and livelihoods from chemical use in agriculture
Getting out of chemical use in agriculture to reduce contaminations of the ecosystems and impacts on human health. OneHealth approaches applied to rodents in Nan. Individual interview and focus group discussions. Participatory workshops, role play games, community engagement, exploration of scenarios toward reducing pesticide use.
Participatory exploration of scenarios out of chemical use: changes in techniques - practices, principles - regulations, policies changes.
Many projects promote alternative practices or behaviors, but their impact remains limited.
Impact pathway requires identifying the real drivers of change: role of knowledge, money, power in addressing wicked problems
Reflections on community engagement and its limits in a context of ageing farming population – questioning intergenerational knowledge transmission patterns
1.3. Biodiversity dynamics between forest and agriculture
Biodiversity is an important element of resilience in the socioecological systems. We propose a holistic approach across multiple scales, sectors and stakeholders to address entangled issues. How the research community engages in wicked problems through concepts such as adaptive spaces, learning centers, living labs, knowledge-scapes. How to maintain a balance between forest and agriculture in complex landscape mosaics. 
Along a gradient of agricultural intensification, we analyze the impacts on biodiversity of agroforestry and community forests, regulatory frameworks, and resource management. From conflicts over resources to reorganization of upland landscapes and livelihoods, we document the changing relations between forest and agriculture through restudy of villages that were studied long ago along a gradient of agricultural intensification from community forestry to intensive monocropping. 

2. Social engagement
2.1. Organic vegetable networks – Sustaining income for smallholders
What is the future of organic farming in Nan Province and what would be the enabling conditions of such a future? How does organic farming link with ecotourism potential of the upland province. We address these questions by investigating the past and the present of organic farming and then by mobilizing this knowledge to explore possible futures with key stakeholders. 
Going organic is not only a question of agronomic practices or economic development. In this research, we investigated the multiple dimensions of organic agriculture, including the underlying values of engagement in alternative practices and the challenges related to the marketing of clean products (certification, etc.). Going organic is also a lifestyle beyond agricultural practices. We study the pathways that brought individuals or collectives to different models of organic farming and explore the diversity of organic farms in Nan province.
2.2. Native Red Cows - Preserving natural and cultural heritage
The Nan Red Cow landrace is a domesticated, locally adapted, native species of cattle that has developed over time, through adaptation to its natural and cultural environment, and due to isolation from other populations of the species. A research-action program started 10 years ago to characterize the genetic strain of the Nan red cow and then to preserve this traditional landrace as an agricultural heritage of Nan Province. Following training sessions, pure breed cows were given to members of a farmers’ network who shared the calves born to other members of the network to gradually improve the genetics of the native cow population.
Our outcome evidencing study investigates the transformative role of the scientific knowledge mobilized by Chulalongkorn University’s Center for Social Engagement in this intervention. It also explores the benefits and constraints related to the different intervention mechanisms to see to what extent they can support the long-term community engagement. The study feeds contemporary scientific debates about transformative research and the role of scientific knowledge in supporting local development.
2.3. La Oon Nan – Sensitizing the youth to biodiversity values
Scientific research conducted on community forests in Nan Province have revealed the value of the ecosystem services they provide. However, this rich body of knowledge still needed to be passed on to the younger generation – to drive their engagement in forest conservation. The research entitled “La Oon Nan or Nan Talents” was implemented by the Faculty of Science of Chulalongkorn University. It created shared learning among youths about community forest management. 
The participatory action-research comprised three main stages: (i) sensitization of school children and local youths on forest multiple values through gaming simulation approaches, (ii) involved community members in monitoring forest resources through direct measurements of carbon and biodiversity, and (iii) shared the results with community members and local authorities through creative communication approaches. The evaluation study of “La Oon Nan” assessed the impact pathway of the transformative pedagogy that was applied by the action-research described here above over a ten-year period in Nan Province.

3. Agroecology emergence
3.1. Learning centers as islands of agroecological coherence
Sustainable agriculture has long been supported by multiple actor networks that operate over a large range of intervention mechanisms. In the context of Nan Province, different sectors, such as private enterprises, government agencies, royal projects, scientific researchers, Buddhist temples, individual farmers or local communities are involved in agricultural extension projects that support alternative agroecological practices. Some initiatives are time bound; others are deeply rooted in specific locations and durably engaged with local communities. 
We consider these learning centers as living laboratories that offer concrete support to experiential learning beyond the traditional activities of agricultural extension services. Our survey of learning centers distributed across the Nan province leads to a description of their historical pathways, social networks, practices, and business models. We study the underlying social networks and higher-level coalitions that may synergize their actions toward an agroecology movement.


3.2. From community engagement to agroecology movement
Agroecology definitions traditionally distinguish three interrelated dimensions: science, practice and movement. This research addresses the third dimension of social engagement toward an agroecological transition. That means going beyond demonstrations and prototyping of alternative agricultural practices to broad-based transformations engaging the whole society.
Nan province has a long history of civil society movement that built an extraordinary culture of community service and trust under resource scarcity. Several scholars have analysed the factors that supported the province-wide movements, including the roles played by local leaders. They emphasized cross-sector collaboration between community, government, business, NGOs, and academics for sustainable development. Many lessons learnt may support current policy dialogues and community engagements towards the emergence of an agroecology movement. 
3.3. Experiential learning for advancing agroecology
In the context of Nan Province, we have shown the key roles of: (i) strong leadership driven by higher values and principles, (ii) adaptive spaces favoring synergies across multiple sectors, and (iii) governance models supportive to knowledge co-creation. They are crucial elements of an agroecological transition capable of dealing with the long list of “wicked problems” that have persisted in time. Today, a large part of the farmers is still dependent on intensive monocropping for their survival and many of them are trapped in the vicious circle of indebtedness. While this agricultural population is ageing and intensive monocropping is still covering large areas of the province, new hopes come in the form of “newcomers” who develop alternative practices better aligned with the principles of agroecology. To support intergenerational solidarity and experiential education, we co-designed two agroecological curriculum dedicated to three types of learners: high school students, community members and university students.
